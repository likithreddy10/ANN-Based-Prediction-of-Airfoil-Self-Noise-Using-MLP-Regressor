## Executive Summary & Abstract

Aeroacoustic self-noise generated by lifting surfaces represents a primary environmental and aerodynamic design constraint across civil aviation, unmanned aerial systems (UAVs), wind turbines, and rotorcraft. Conventional computational fluid dynamics (CFD) leveraging Large Eddy Simulations (LES) or Direct Numerical Simulations (DNS) demand millions of CPU core hours per operating flight point, severely limiting rapid multi-disciplinary design optimization (MDO) and real-time active aeroacoustic control.

This project delivers an end-to-end, fully reproducible Artificial Neural Network (ANN) regression solution utilizing a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP Regressor) optimized exclusively via **Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD)**. Trained on 1,503 aerodynamic and acoustic observations from the NASA Langley Anechoic Noise Facility wind-tunnel tests across NACA 0012 blade sections (UCI Dataset ID 291), the network models the non-linear transfer function from five fluid and geometric features to the scaled sound pressure level ($y$ in decibels, dB).

We present a comprehensive empirical benchmark comparing a shallow single-layer baseline model ($R^2 = 0.8253$, $\text{RMSE} = 2.96\text{ dB}$) against a proposed deep hierarchical network ($256 \to 128 \to 64 \to 32$) that achieves an exceptional **$R^2 = 0.9749$** and **$\text{RMSE} = 1.12\text{ dB}$** on an unseen 20% holdout test partition (301 samples). Controlled ablation experiments across learning rates ($\eta \in [0.001, 0.05]$), mini-batch sizes ($B \in [16, 256]$), and activation functions (ReLU, Tanh, and Logistic) elucidate critical optimization dynamics, including target-scaling gradient stabilization and activation vanishing gradients.

## 1. Problem Statement

### 1.1 Engineering Context
Airfoil self-noise is defined as the sound produced when an airfoil blade interacts with undisturbed, non-turbulent freestream airflow. The noise originates entirely from turbulence generated within the boundary layer and near wake of the lifting surface. As turbulent boundary layer eddies pass the sharp trailing edge, surface pressure fluctuations scatter into radiating acoustic pressure waves, creating broadband trailing-edge noise, laminar boundary-layer vortex shedding, separation-stall noise, and trailing-edge bluntness vortex noise.

### 1.2 Computational Challenges
1. **CFD Intractability:** Resolving high-frequency acoustic waves ($f \le 20\text{ kHz}$) within Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) is unviable; unsteady LES requires spatial grid resolutions on the order of the Kolmogorov scale near the wall ($\Delta y^+ \approx 1$), requiring weeks of supercomputing time.
2. **Semi-Empirical Model Deficiencies:** Classical acoustic formulation methods (such as the Brooks, Pope, and Marcolini / BPM model) rely on empirical curve-fits that exhibit significant prediction errors when boundary layers experience moderate separation or adverse pressure gradients.
3. **Non-Linear Multi-Parameter Coupling:** Acoustic sound pressure level does not vary linearly with blade geometry or flow velocity; it couples non-linearly with the Strouhal number ($St = \frac{f \delta^*}{U_\infty}$) and boundary layer thickness ($\delta^*$).

### 1.3 Technical Project Goal
Develop, train, optimize, and evaluate an Artificial Neural Network regression model using a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) architecture trained strictly via **Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD)**. The project must preprocess the raw aerodynamic data, conduct controlled experiments, evaluate performance on unseen test data using standard regression metrics ($\text{MAE}$, $\text{MSE}$, $\text{RMSE}$, $R^2$), and analyze physical limitations and error characteristics.

## 3. Data Preprocessing & Reproducibility Setup

### 3.1 Partitioning Protocol
To evaluate true model generalization on unseen data, the 1,503 observations were split into:
- **Training Set:** 80% (1,202 samples)
- **Holdout Test Set:** 20% (301 samples)
- **Random Seed:** Strictly fixed at `random_state = 42` across all splits, weight initializations, and mini-batch shufflers to ensure full scientific reproducibility.

### 3.2 Input Feature Standardization
Because input attributes span drastically different orders of magnitude (Frequency up to $20,000\text{ Hz}$ vs. displacement thickness of $\approx 0.0004\text{ m}$), feeding raw values into a neural network skews the loss surface, forcing gradient descent into pathological oscillations.

Z-score standardization was applied:
$$z_j^{(i)} = \frac{x_j^{(i)} - \mu_j}{\sigma_j}$$
Where $\mu_j$ and $\sigma_j$ were computed exclusively from the 1,202 training samples and then used to transform both the training and test sets.

### 3.3 Target Scaling: Preventing Exploding Gradients in SGD
In regression with Stochastic Gradient Descent, calculating output layer errors with raw decibel values ($y \approx 103 - 141\text{ dB}$) results in initial output errors:
$$\delta^{(L)} = (\hat{y}^{(i)} - y^{(i)}) \approx -125$$
With learning rates $\eta \ge 0.01$, weight updates explode on epoch 1, producing non-finite / `NaN` weights.

**Solution:** Standardize target variable $y$ during training:
$$y_{scaled} = \frac{y - \mu_y}{\sigma_y}, \quad \mu_y = 124.84\text{ dB}, \quad \sigma_y = 6.90\text{ dB}$$
At test time, the model's standardized prediction $\hat{y}_{scaled}$ is inverse-transformed back to physical decibels:
$$\hat{y}_{dB} = \hat{y}_{scaled} \cdot \sigma_y + \mu_y$$
This simple transformation stabilized SGD optimization across all architectures and learning rates.

## 5. Optimization & Training Algorithm (SGD)

### 5.1 Objective Loss Function
The network is trained to minimize the regularized Mean Squared Error over mini-batches of size $B$:
$$\mathcal{J}(W, b) = \frac{1}{2B} \sum_{i=1}^B \left( \hat{y}^{(i)} - y^{(i)} \right)^2 + \frac{\alpha}{2} \sum_{l=1}^L \|W^{(l)}\|_F^2$$

### 5.2 Backpropagation Gradients
1. **Output Layer Error:**
   $$\delta^{(L)} = \nabla_{\hat{y}} \mathcal{J} = (\hat{y} - y)$$
2. **Hidden Layer Error Backpropagation:**
   $$\delta^{(l)} = \left[ (W^{(l+1)})^T \delta^{(l+1)} \right] \odot \sigma'(z^{(l)})$$
   For ReLU activation, $\sigma'(z^{(l)}) = \mathbf{1}_{\{z^{(l)} > 0\}}$.
3. **Weight Gradients:**
   $$\frac{\partial \mathcal{J}}{\partial W^{(l)}} = \frac{1}{B} \delta^{(l)} (a^{(l-1)})^T + \alpha W^{(l)}$$

### 5.3 Stochastic Gradient Descent with Nesterov Accelerated Gradient (NAG)
To accelerate convergence through flat plateaus and damp oscillations in narrow ravines, Nesterov Accelerated Gradient momentum ($\beta = 0.9$) was employed:
$$v_{t+1} = \beta \cdot v_t + \eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}(\theta_t - \beta v_t)$$
$$\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - v_{t+1}$$

### 5.4 Adaptive Learning Rate Schedule
The learning rate initializes at $\eta_0 = 0.01$. If the loss fails to decrease by at least $10^{-4}$ for two consecutive epochs, the learning rate is scaled down by a factor of 5 ($\eta \leftarrow \eta / 5$). Training includes early stopping with a patience of 25 epochs.

## 7. Discussion

### 7.1 Stochastic Noise as an Implicit Regularizer
The batch size ablation in Table 3 illustrates a fundamental property of Stochastic Gradient Descent: smaller mini-batches ($B = 16, 32$) systematically outperformed larger mini-batches ($B = 256$) by $0.025$ in $R^2$. In small mini-batches, the gradient calculated over 16 randomly drawn samples has high variance relative to the true full-batch gradient. This stochastic noise prevents the optimizer from settling into narrow, sharp local minima, biasing the trajectory toward flat minima that exhibit lower generalization error on test data.

### 7.2 Demonstration of the Vanishing Gradient Problem
The comparison between ReLU and Logistic (Sigmoid) activations in Table 4 provides textbook empirical evidence of the vanishing gradient phenomenon. For the Logistic function:
$$\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}, \quad \sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z)) \le 0.25$$
Backpropagating through four layers scales the error gradient by:
$$\prod_{l=1}^4 \sigma'(z^{(l)}) \le (0.25)^4 = 0.0039$$
The gradients reaching the first hidden layer are diminished by over $99.6\%$, preventing the network from learning features from the raw inputs. Consequently, the network predicted the mean target value for all inputs, resulting in $R^2 = 0.0041$. In contrast, ReLU possesses a derivative of exactly 1 for all positive activations ($\text{ReLU}'(z) = 1$ when $z > 0$), preserving gradient magnitude throughout backpropagation.

### 7.3 Residual Distribution & Physical Validation
Analyzing the residual errors ($e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$) on the test set:
- Mean Error ($\mu_e$): $-0.02\text{ dB}$ (essentially zero mean, confirming no systematic bias).
- Standard Deviation ($\sigma_e$): $1.12\text{ dB}$.
- Over 92% of all test predictions fall within $\pm 1.5\text{ dB}$ of actual experimental wind tunnel measurements, which is within the experimental measurement uncertainty of the microphone array itself ($\approx \pm 1.5\text{ dB}$).

## 8. Practical Limitations

1. **Airfoil Profile Specificity:** The empirical dataset was gathered exclusively on symmetric NACA 0012 profiles. Highly cambered airfoils (such as NACA 4412 or supercritical commercial transport airfoils) feature asymmetric pressure distributions and laminar separation bubbles that require additional geometric camber and thickness parameters.
2. **Subsonic Limitation:** Wind tunnel flow velocity in the dataset ranges from $31.7\text{ m/s}$ to $71.3\text{ m/s}$ ($M \le 0.21$). Transonic shock buffet noise, supersonic trailing-edge shocklets, and compressible compressibility effects are absent from the training manifold.
3. **Inflow Turbulence Assumption:** The NASA Langley wind tunnel has a smooth, anechoic inflow with low freestream turbulence intensity ($Tu < 0.1\%$). In real-world atmospheric environments (such as wind turbines operating in the atmospheric boundary layer), leading-edge turbulent ingestion noise often equals or exceeds trailing-edge self-noise.
